> What does someone need from you before they are willing to go deeper?
{: .prompt-tip }

*Note: This post responds to feedback on an earlier piece, [How's work? Busy lah.](/posts/hows-work-busy-lah/), but it stands on its own. That piece was about what to ask. This one is about when to ask and how far to go. What we owe someone after they open up deserves its own discussion and I will return to it in a later piece.*

## The feedback

After I published a post on asking better questions, a close friend wrote to me with a thoughtful response. He understood the aim and the method. He then raised a question I had not considered.

> Can this be generalized to all conversation that has the potential to go deep?

His concern came from a recent realisation. He believed that he and I are what he called **intense** people. We almost always prefer a conversation to go as deep as possible. In his observation most people are not like this. Not everyone can move from zero to a hundred at once. He pointed to the small talk culture in the United States as an example. People there begin at zero and gradually move toward more meaningful ground. They do this through related topics and other skills that he admitted he had not yet understood.

His question deserves a careful answer. The word he chose is a good place to begin.

I understand why he called me intense. I am not afraid of conversations that others find exposing. Given the choice I will usually take the deeper road. Several people have told me that I come across as genuine and that they find themselves sharing more than they intended. Yet intense is not quite the right word. It suggests speed or force. Neither of these is what makes a conversation go deep. People open up when they feel **safe**. What my friend noticed is a willingness to enter vulnerable ground combined with an effort to make that ground safe for the other person.

This reframes his question. Most people are not slow. They simply need evidence of safety before they move further. Each person gathers that evidence at their own pace.

Small talk is where much of that evidence is gathered. In my earlier post I treated it as the thing that wasted the hour. I now believe that view was incomplete.

> How's work? Busy lah.

On the surface this exchange carries no information. Beneath it both people are quietly assessing each other. Is the other person tired or guarded today? Is it safe to say more? The problem is not that small talk happens. The problem arises when a conversation never leaves it. Small talk is the runway that a deeper conversation needs before it can take off.

In Hong Kong that runway is short. The city runs on speed and efficiency. Small talk among friends often takes the form of good-natured teasing that leads nowhere unless someone deliberately steers it elsewhere. A reflective question asks people to slow down and think. In a city built on pace this can easily feel like a waste of time.

The experiences that follow have shaped how I now think about his question.

## The step back

Some time ago I shared a one-on-one meal with someone I was only beginning to know. Early in the evening I asked a few personal questions. The answers were brief and hesitant. I therefore stepped back and asked about lighter matters from their own world. These were subjects they were clearly happy to discuss. Later in the evening we returned to deeper ground and this time the conversation went there.

In hindsight this meal contained the skill my friend could not name. When a deeper question met resistance I neither pushed nor gave up. I moved instead to a nearby subject that felt safe. This is what he meant by related topics. The step back was not a retreat. It was the means by which trust was built within a single evening.

I have come to think of people as onions. No one reaches the centre by cutting through. One is admitted a layer at a time and the other person decides when. Some people reveal several layers in an evening. Others reveal one in a year. Most keep a few that no one ever sees.

## When I was the onion

I am not always the one making room. I have layers of my own and others have had to wait for them too.

A while ago, I travelled with a group of close friends to a new city. We stayed together and spent our days exploring without much of a plan. During those days I found myself discussing matters I had never raised with them in Hong Kong. They were the same friends I saw regularly at home. What had changed was the setting. We were away from our routines and sharing something new. Each of us knew the others were there for us. Opening up felt natural and the openness was mutual.

On another occasion I was sharing a meal with a friend. For most of it we spoke about how life was going and other everyday matters. When the plates were cleared a short silence followed. Without consciously deciding to, I began to speak about something personal. What remained with me was his response. He began to share as well and continued for another fifteen or twenty minutes after we had finished eating. He told me things I had never heard from him before.

I had not planned that moment. I had simply gone first. I have observed this pattern many times since. When one person takes the risk of honesty the other usually meets them there.

## The evening I was not ready

Honesty about depth has to run in both directions.

There have been evenings when I met a friend while my own energy was low and did not say so. On one of them it became clear partway through the meal that my friend was carrying something heavy. The conversation needed my full attention. I wanted to give it but had little left.

Nothing went seriously wrong. Still I left feeling I had not been as present as I would have wanted to be. Had I been honest about my state at the outset we could have chosen a lighter evening or returned to the subject another day. The experience taught me that openness to depth is not the same as readiness for it. If I neglect my own energy I cannot truly be present for anyone else. Reading the room includes reading myself.

It also taught me the value of stating the purpose of a meeting. Not every meal serves the same end. Some are simple catch-ups. Some offer company to someone who is lonely. Others are meant for a real conversation. My close friends already expect a one-on-one with me to be meaningful. When I plan a longer day with someone I say so plainly. I mention that it would be good to talk about life and the stage we are each at, the challenges we face and how I might help. Stating this at the outset ensures that no one is caught off guard when the conversation turns.

## The silence in the hospital room

One of the deepest conversations I have had began with nothing at all.

A friend of mine was in hospital. I imagined myself in his position and knew I would be grateful for someone close by throughout the day. In difficult times the presence of a familiar person means a great deal to me. I therefore took my laptop and worked from his room. By evening the day had grown quiet. We had finished eating. I was clearing the table and checking my laptop while he rested in bed. For five or ten minutes neither of us spoke.

Without any prompting he then returned to a topic we had touched on earlier. We had agreed to revisit it someday. I had not expected it to come that day or in that room. He shared something deeply personal. He then asked me some honest questions of his own and I answered as truthfully as I could. I had not asked a single question to begin it. I was simply present and grateful to be so.

Every principle I had developed assumed that depth is something one builds. That evening it was something I allowed. The hospital room was quiet and private. The conversation arrived in a silence that no one rushed to fill.

## My reply

Here is my answer to my friend.

The approach does not generalise to every conversation. Good questions show where a conversation can go. Small talk is the runway. Safety is what allows anyone to take off. Different people need runways of different lengths. Some have opened up before and been hurt. Others have met enough unkind people that unexpected warmth makes them wary. A long runway is not a flaw in such a person. It reflects what their life has taught them.

The skill he has not yet named is less mysterious than it appears. When a deeper question meets resistance, step back to something nearby that feels safe. Be honest about why you are meeting and about how much you have to give that day. Be willing to go first. Sometimes simply remain in the room and stay quiet long enough.

Making it safe does not end when the meal does. When someone opens up, they are lending me something precious. It still belongs to them. I should not pass it on to others. I should not twist it to paint a picture of them, even if we one day drift apart. Without this promise, everything above is just a set of tricks to get people talking. With it, their trust is something I have earned.

I would also question the word he used for the two of us: **intense**. We now live in different countries and our time together is limited. Before we speak we sometimes note down a few things from our lives that we want to share. It is not a formal agenda. We do not stop at what happened. We ask what each experience taught us. Recently he walked me through a subject he had been researching in depth. I learned more in that conversation than I had in years. From the outside this may look intense but from the inside it feels **intentional**. We know our time is short and we choose to spend it on what matters.

> Depth cannot be rushed. It can only be made safe. Ask the good question when the room is ready, step back when it is not, and sometimes just sit with someone long enough for them to choose.
